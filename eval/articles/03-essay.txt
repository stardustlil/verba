Learning Spanish at Forty

I signed up for my first Spanish class on a whim. A friend had talked me into it over dinner, and by the time I had second thoughts, I had already paid the fee. I was forty years old, and I had not set foot in a classroom since college.

The first few weeks were humbling, to say the least. Words that I had memorized the night before slipped my mind the moment the teacher called on me. The teenagers in the back row picked up new phrases with no apparent effort, while I struggled to keep up. More than once I was on the verge of giving up.

What kept me going was a piece of advice from the teacher herself. "Stop trying to be perfect," she told me after class one evening. "Make mistakes on purpose if you have to. Nobody learns to swim by reading about water." I took her words to heart. From then on I made a point of speaking up, even when I was sure I would get it wrong.

Little by little, things began to fall into place. I started to catch the gist of songs on the radio. I could order a meal without resorting to English. One afternoon I found myself chatting with a taxi driver about the weather, and it dawned on me that I had not translated a single sentence in my head.

I am still a long way from fluent, and I have come to terms with the fact that I will always have an accent. But the experience has taught me something that has nothing to do with grammar. For most of my adult life I had steered clear of anything I was not already good at. Learning a language as a beginner reminded me that being bad at something is simply the first step toward being good at it.
