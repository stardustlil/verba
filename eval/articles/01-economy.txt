Remote Work Is Reshaping Small Cities

For decades, ambitious graduates took it for granted that building a career meant moving to a big city. That assumption is starting to fall apart. In the wake of the pandemic, millions of office workers discovered that they could do their jobs from anywhere, and a growing number of them have no intention of going back.

Small cities have been quick to take advantage of the shift. Some offer cash incentives to newcomers, while others have poured money into fast internet and co-working spaces. Local officials argue that remote workers bring in outside income without putting pressure on the job market, and early evidence suggests they have a point. In several towns, new cafes and gyms have sprung up to cater to people who work from home.

Not everyone is better off, however. Housing prices have soared in the most popular destinations, and longtime residents are struggling to make ends meet. A schoolteacher who has lived in the same neighborhood for twenty years may suddenly find herself priced out of it. Critics warn that growth is coming at the expense of the very people who gave these places their character.

Employers, for their part, are having second thoughts. A handful of large firms have ordered staff back to the office, claiming that collaboration suffers when teams never meet face to face. Whether those orders will stick remains to be seen. Workers who have rearranged their lives around flexibility are unlikely to give it up without a fight, and in a tight labor market they still have the upper hand.

In the long run, the outcome will probably fall somewhere in between. Most analysts expect hybrid arrangements to become the norm, with employees splitting their time between home and headquarters. If that turns out to be true, small cities within driving distance of major hubs stand to gain the most.
