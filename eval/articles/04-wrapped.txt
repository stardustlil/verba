The Quiet Return of the City Tram

Half a century ago, most cities tore up their tram lines to make
room for cars. Planners at the time were convinced that private
vehicles were the wave of the future, and public transit fell out of
favor. Today the tide has turned. From Europe to East Asia, local
governments are laying new tracks, and passengers are coming back in
record numbers.

The reasons are not hard to find. Traffic jams cost commuters hours
every week, and air pollution takes a heavy toll on public health.
Trams run on electricity, carry far more people than buses, and are
well-suited to dense city centers where space is at a premium. Shop
owners, who once feared that construction would drive customers
away, have largely changed their minds after seeing foot traffic
rise along the new routes.

Still, building a tram network is no small feat. Costs often run
over budget, and projects can drag on for years. Critics point out
that the money might be better spent on cheaper alter-
natives, such as dedicated bus lanes. Supporters counter that trams
pay for themselves in the long term by raising property values and
attracting invest-
ment along their routes.

For now, the trend shows no sign of slowing down. Cities that took
the plunge early are reaping the benefits, and others are eager to
follow suit.
