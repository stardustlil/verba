Why a Good Night's Sleep Helps You Remember

Anyone who has stayed up all night before an exam knows that tired brains do not work well. What scientists have only recently begun to understand is why. A growing body of research suggests that sleep plays a crucial role in turning fragile new memories into lasting ones.

During the day, the brain takes in far more information than it can keep. At night, it sorts through the pile. Brain scans show that patterns of activity recorded while a person learns a task are replayed during deep sleep, as if the brain were going over its notes. Memories that are replayed tend to stick, while the rest gradually fade away.

In one well-known experiment, volunteers were asked to memorize a list of word pairs. Half of them slept for eight hours afterward; the other half were kept awake. When both groups were tested two days later, those who had slept outperformed the others by a wide margin. The gap did not close even after the sleep-deprived group had caught up on rest.

Sleep also seems to help the brain make sense of what it has learned. People who sleep on a difficult problem are more likely to come up with a creative solution, perhaps because rest allows the mind to draw connections between ideas that seem unrelated at first glance.

None of this means that more sleep is always better, and researchers are careful not to jump to conclusions. Still, the evidence points in one direction: cutting back on sleep to gain extra study time is likely to do more harm than good. For students under pressure, the best advice may also be the simplest. Close the book, turn off the light, and let the brain do its job.
